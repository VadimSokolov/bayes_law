# Chapter 7. Standards of Proof as Decision Rules

This chapter reads standards of proof as decision thresholds on the posterior probability, fixed by the ratio of the losses from false conviction and false acquittal, following Kaplan's decision theoretic analysis. It covers proof beyond reasonable doubt, the balance of probabilities and clear and convincing evidence, and the numerical equivalents judges give, as in United States v Fatico. It treats the seriousness of an allegation in Re H (Minors) and Re B (Children), read as a point about the prior and not the threshold, the presumption of innocence read as a 1/N prior, and the gatecrasher paradox, the blue bus case, naked statistical evidence and proportional remedies, with loss of a chance in Allied Maples v Simmons and Simmons and Gregg v Scott. It then turns to Lindley's graduated sanctions, the value of information and when a court should stop and decide, the conjunction paradox of Cohen against element by element proof, relative plausibility, and Scotland's not proven verdict and its abolition. It argues that the paradoxes reflect the all or nothing form of the law more than a failure of probability.
