This chapter examines order without effective state courts through two bodies of work. Roger Gould studied vendetta in nineteenth century Corsica using French court records and found that revenge was rare and was a targeted sanction against collective aggression, a display of solidarity, not blind collective responsibility. Gould's Collision of Wills (2003) argues that conflict is most likely where social rank is ambiguous, which the chapter reads as disagreement about a posterior, alongside Priest and Klein on litigation and Fearon on war. Diego Gambetta analysed the Sicilian mafia as an industry selling private protection, with reputation and costly signals as its capital, and economists have tested origins in land fragmentation, sulphur and citrus exports. The chapter covers Article 416 bis, Tommaso Buscetta and Giovanni Falcone, the maxi trial in Palermo, omerta, pentiti, and the weight of accomplice testimony under Article 192 of the Italian code of criminal procedure and the Marino judgment (1992), with likelihood ratios and corroboration. It ends with network inference and lessons for law.
