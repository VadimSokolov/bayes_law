# Chapter 2. Bayesian Decisions for Lawyers

From *Bayes and Law: Evidence, Expected Utility and Machines in the Courtroom* by Nick Polson and Vadim Sokolov (2026), chapter 2. This chapter presents Bayesian decision theory for lawyers as four questions: what are the actions, what are the states of the world, what is each consequence worth, and how probable is each state given the evidence. It builds the loss table for a criminal verdict with false conviction loss c_I and false acquittal loss c_II, and connects it to the classical language of Type I and Type II errors, significance level, power, and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. It derives the Bayes risk of a decision rule, shows that the optimal error rate depends on the base rate and the loss ratio rather than a fixed five per cent, and shows that the posterior threshold rule applied case by case minimises aggregate risk. A worked example shows how a one per cent false positive rate can still give about one innocent person in twenty three among positive results. The chapter extends the framework to many actions, continuous decisions and damages (posterior mean under squared error, posterior median under absolute error, asymmetric loss and quantiles), sequential decisions, the costs of process and the value of accuracy, and deterrence and incentives. Worked examples cover bail decisions and the choice whether to sue under the American rule, the English costs rule (CPR 44.2) and qualified one way costs shifting.
