How should courts measure skill and chance, and what does a Bayesian expert witness do? The chapter follows Joseph Kadane's work as a consultant and expert. It presents the dominant factor, material element and any chance tests for gambling, Kadane's comparison of dumb and smart strategies for electronic draw poker machines, and a random effects model in which the skill share of variance rises with the number of hands, so that the answer depends on the horizon the law adopts. It covers poker litigation (Joker Club v Hardin, United States v DiCristina), daily fantasy sports (White v Cuomo, New York 2022), and Indian law on rummy and horse racing (Satyanarayana 1968, Lakshmanan 1996) and the 2025 statute on online money games. It then surveys his other engagements: jury challenges (Azania v State), peremptory strikes and Batson v Kentucky, driving while black and State v Soto with missing data, hierarchical models for employment discrimination and Wal Mart v Dukes, and sampling in tax audits. It closes with four lessons for expert witnesses: define the question in the law's terms, use simple and robust models, be open about the prior, and accept that courts may decide on other grounds.
