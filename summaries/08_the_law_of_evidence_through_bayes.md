# Chapter 8. The Law of Evidence Through Bayes

This chapter reads the main doctrines of the law of evidence in English and American law as responses to problems of inference, asking what a Bayesian account says about each. Evidence acts through its likelihood ratio, and exclusionary rules, warnings and directions aim to reduce errors in judging the ratio or the prior. The topics are relevance and probative value (Federal Rule of Evidence 403, section 78 of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984), with a model of jury overweighting of evidence, character and bad character (Criminal Justice Act 2003, R v Hanson), hearsay and its exceptions (Crawford v Washington), identification evidence (R v Turnbull, line ups, witness confidence), confessions and false confessions, corroboration (including Scots law), expert evidence (Daubert, Rule 702, error rates, the PCAST report) and the ultimate issue, privileges and exclusionary rules, and jury directions such as the lies direction. The chapter argues that these rules can be understood as tools for managing uncertain likelihood ratios, and that each trades accuracy against other values.
