# Chapter 6. Fallacies of Classical Inference

This chapter asks which misreadings of classical statistics recur in litigation and what a Bayesian analysis of the same data would conclude. It builds on Finkelstein and Levin's Statistics for Lawyers and sets out ten fallacies, each with a statement, an explanation and a worked example: reading the p value as the probability that the null is true, treating non significance as no effect, equating significance with importance, misreading confidence intervals as probability statements, ignoring multiple comparisons (the Texas sharpshooter), the dependence of p values on the stopping rule and the likelihood principle, the two or three standard deviations rule of Castaneda and Hazelwood, one tailed versus two tailed tests, combining p values (Fisher's method, Lucia de Berk), and testing without an alternative (People v Collins). The cases include Matrixx Initiatives v Siracusano and Milward v Acuity. It argues that the absence of an explicit prior is what produces the fallacies, and that the remedy is to state the prior and show how the conclusion depends on it. The worked examples are constructed to be realistic, and some follow the pattern of decided cases.
