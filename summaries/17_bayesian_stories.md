Does storytelling in the courtroom conflict with probabilistic reasoning, and how can a narrative be scored as evidence? The chapter argues that a story is a generative model of how the evidence arose, so that comparing stories is Bayesian model comparison. The three criteria of the Pennington and Hastie story model, coverage, coherence and uniqueness, are mapped to the likelihood, the prior and the concentration of the posterior, and the integral over a story's free parameters supplies a Bayesian Occam's razor against flexible stories. Weight of evidence is measured in decibans, and the posterior probability of guilt is written as a sum over all guilty and innocent stories, which shows why comparing only the two stories the parties tell can mislead, as in Lord Brandon's remark in The Popi M. It then works through the murder of Meredith Kercher in Perugia (Amanda Knox, Raffaele Sollecito, Rudy Guede): the lone assailant story against the joint story, illustrative decibans for each item of evidence (DNA on the knife and the bra clasp, the absence of Knox traces, the broken window, the statements), a prior, and a posterior range. The authors present the numbers as illustrations, not findings. The chapter argues that the case turned on the probability of DNA contamination, and it closes with the use of large language models to generate and score stories.

The theory of stories as generative models is the authors' own (Polson and Maharaj 2025).
