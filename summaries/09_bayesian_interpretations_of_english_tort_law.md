Does English negligence law have a Bayesian structure? The chapter reads the reasonable person as a Bayesian agent whose beliefs and precautions are judged against the evidence available. It works through breach (probability, severity, cost of precaution and social value in Bolton v Stone, Paris v Stepney, Latimer v AEC and Watt v Hertfordshire), the Hand formula B less than PL, professional standards (Bolam), and informed consent as an expected utility or value of information test (Montgomery). It then treats causation: the but for test, the doubling of risk rule for epidemiological evidence (Sienkiewicz), material contribution and the Fairchild exception for mesothelioma, and loss of a chance (Hotson, Gregg v Scott). It also covers remoteness (Wagon Mound), duty of care (Donoghue v Stevenson, Caparo), contributory negligence and strict liability (Rylands v Fletcher). The chapter argues that foreseeability is a probability judgment, that causation is a counterfactual probability, and that proportional recovery for lost chances is the Bayesian answer to Lord Hoffmann's deterministic view. Exercises include a gamma Poisson analysis of Bolton v Stone and a Fairchild apportionment calculation.
