This appendix suggests how to teach from the book. It proposes three paths: a fifteen week law school seminar on evidence and probability, given as a week by week schedule that pairs chapters with leading cases such as Collins, Clark, Adams, Matrixx, Re B, Turnbull, Hanson, Daubert, Gregg v Scott, Fairchild, Wells v Wells and Twombly; a course for statisticians and economists that emphasises models and computation, Bayes factors, graphical models and generative Bayesian computation; and a two day short course for judges, prosecutors and practitioners built from selected chapters on odds, the island problem, standards of proof, evidence, clusters, intuition and machines. A final section describes forms of assessment: short memoranda on the Notes and Questions, a case analysis, and a reform proposal. No prior statistics is assumed beyond Appendix A. An instructor uses it to plan a syllabus.

## From the literature

The syllabus cites leading cases covered in the chapters: R v Collins, R v Clark, R v Adams, Matrixx Initiatives v Siracusano, Re B, R v Turnbull, Daubert v Merrell Dow, Gregg v Scott, Fairchild v Glenhaven and Wells v Wells, and Bell Atlantic v Twombly (2007).
