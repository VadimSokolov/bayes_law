Should machines decide, and how should courts use artificial intelligence? The chapter restates Lindley's challenge: if a court combines probabilities and utilities coherently, that task is a computation. It surveys what machines already do: actuarial risk assessment and State v Loomis, prediction of decisions (Aletras and colleagues, Katz, Bommarito and Blackman), technology assisted review in Da Silva Moore v Publicis Groupe and Pyrrho Investments v MWB Property, hallucinated citations in Mata v Avianca and Ayinde v Haringey, and online courts. It then describes ten channels of change: triage, drafting, evidence analysis, deepfakes and the likelihood ratio of recordings, settlement under the Priest and Klein model, decision support, consistency and noise, access to justice, verification, and the role of the judge. It treats fairness and calibration, including the impossibility results of Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan and Chouldechova, the COMPAS dispute, and the selective labels problem, and closes with eight requirements for a Bayesian machine in court.
