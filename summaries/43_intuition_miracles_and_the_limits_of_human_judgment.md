How should a fact finder weigh testimony and opinion, and how reliable is human intuition? The chapter begins with Hume on miracles in the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748) and reads his maxim as a statement about posterior odds: testimony establishes an extraordinary event only if its falsehood would be more improbable than the event. It discusses Earman's criticism, that Hume neglected independent witnesses, and applies the maxim to serious allegations, linking it to Lord Nicholls in Re H and to the medical murder cases. It then reviews heuristics and biases (Tversky and Kahneman), anchoring among judges (Englich, Mussweiler and Strack), the conditions under which intuition works (Kahneman and Klein), Meehl on clinical versus statistical prediction, Dawes on improper linear models, and the Kleinberg bail study. It states Lindley's point about coherence and ends with a six step procedure for weighing testimony using priors, likelihood ratios, shared sources and thresholds.
