This chapter asks what the justice system costs and how its scarce resources should be allocated. It starts from the idea that justice is a scarce resource, and then examines the value of accuracy in adjudication (Kaplow's question, the trade off between accuracy and cost, and accuracy about what). It treats the docket as a portfolio, judges as a cost of the system, and the cost of delay. It discusses appeals as error correction, rules on who pays costs (the English and American rules, proportionality and costs management, legal aid), the measurement of system performance, and judges as decision makers. The method is decision theoretic: the benefit of additional accuracy is weighed against the resources it consumes, and the same reasoning is applied to allocating judges' time and to designing appeals. Readers searching for law and economics of procedure, court congestion, case management, fee shifting, costs budgeting, legal aid funding, or error correction in appeals will find the chapter's argument and its sources here.

## From the literature

The value of accuracy in adjudication (Kaplow 1994); accuracy in the assessment of damages (Kaplow and Shavell 1996); the appeals process as a means of error correction (Shavell 1995). The English and American rules on costs, proportionality and costs management, and legal aid are presented as background doctrine.
