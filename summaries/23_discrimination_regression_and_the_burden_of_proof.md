# Chapter 23. Discrimination, Regression and the Burden of Proof

This chapter asks how courts use statistics to infer discriminatory intent and how regression evidence and burdens of proof structure that inference. It covers disparate treatment and disparate impact under Title VII and the Equality Act 2010, the four fifths rule, and a Bayesian posterior for the ratio of selection rates. It treats regression in pattern and practice cases, omitted variable bias, sensitivity analysis, and the bad control or mediator problem, using a causal graph. It reads Students for Fair Admissions v Harvard as a dispute between two logistic regression analyses, by Arcidiacono and Card, that differed on the personal rating, special categories of applicants and pooling, and argues that the experts were reporting posteriors under different causal models. It reads McDonnell Douglas and Teamsters burden shifting as a way of structuring Bayesian updating, with a burden of production on the employer and the burden of persuasion on the plaintiff. It ends with algorithmic hiring and credit models, calibration within groups, and the search for less discriminatory alternatives.
