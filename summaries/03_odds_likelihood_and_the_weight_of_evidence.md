# Chapter 3. Odds, Likelihood and the Weight of Evidence

This chapter develops the likelihood ratio as the currency of evidence and applies it to statistical errors in criminal trials. It explains why the likelihood ratio is comparative, separates the expert from the fact finder, and multiplies for independent items so that Good's weight of evidence in decibans adds. It works through the prosecutor's fallacy and the island problem formula, the defence fallacy, the independence fallacy in People v Collins, and the Sally Clark case, where the correct comparison is between double murder and double sudden infant death. It reworks R v Adams, in which Peter Donnelly presented Bayes' theorem to the jury and the Court of Appeal was hostile, and covers R v Doheny and Adams on the presentation of DNA evidence, and R v T on footwear marks and the limits of likelihood ratios. Further sections read relevance (DPP v Kilbourne, Federal Rule of Evidence 401) as a likelihood ratio, read Re B (Children) on the binary standard of proof, treat a trial as a sequential probability ratio test in the manner of Turing and Wald, discuss verbal scales such as that of ENFSI, the cab problem and base rate neglect, and the blue bus case Smith v Rapid Transit on naked statistics.
