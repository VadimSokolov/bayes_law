# Chapter 30. Africa as a Natural Experiment

This chapter treats Africa as a natural laboratory for the legal origins thesis, since colonial powers transplanted common law, French civil law, Roman Dutch law and other systems, alongside Islamic and customary law. It explains why law cannot be separated from the rest of the colonial package, and uses border designs and regression discontinuity (Michalopoulos and Papaioannou on partitioned ethnic homelands) to isolate colonial effects. Cameroon, a bijural state divided between British and French mandates, is the sharpest case (Lee and Schultz 2012). The chapter proposes a hierarchical Bayesian border model with Gaussian process smooth terms and border level covariates. It then discusses OHADA harmonised business law and its Common Court of Justice and Arbitration, the Anglophone crisis and the lawyers' strike of 2016, customary law and the repugnancy clause as an evidence problem, annulment of presidential elections by the Supreme Court of Kenya (2017) and the Constitutional Court of Malawi (2020), and how slow courts affect contracts and development.

## From the literature

Michalopoulos and Papaioannou (2013, 2014); Lee and Schultz (2012) on Cameroon; Klerman, Mahoney, Spamann and Weinstein (2011) on legal origin and colonial history; Djankov and coauthors (2003) on Courts; the Kenya Supreme Court annulment of the 2017 presidential election; the Malawi Constitutional Court annulment of the 2019 election. The border model, the decision rule for annulment and the discussion of customary law evidence are the chapter's own worked analysis, presented as illustration.
