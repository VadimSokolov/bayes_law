# Chapter 1. Why the Law Needs Bayes

This chapter asks why a court, which must both find facts and act on them, is best understood as a Bayesian decision maker. It sets out the three rules of probability (convexity, addition, multiplication), Bayes' rule in probability and odds form, the likelihood ratio or Bayes factor, and the expected loss threshold for conviction, with the Blackstone ratio of ten to one giving a posterior threshold of 10/11. It traces the history of probability in law from Jacob and Nicolas Bernoulli, Leibniz, Condorcet's jury theorem and Poisson's work on French juries, through Mill's hostility, Wigmore's charts, and Turing and Good's weight of evidence, to the Finkelstein and Fairley proposal and Tribe's Trial by Mathematics. It presents Lindley's challenge that a court is a computation combining probabilities and utilities, and answers objections such as the presumption of innocence, single event probabilities, and juries that cannot do arithmetic, using R v Adams as an illustration. It then previews the economics of courts, legal AI, formal verification in Lean and Catala, comparative common and civil law reasoning, legal origins, and order without the state. The chapter argues that decision theory under uncertainty has one coherent form and that it fits the law.
