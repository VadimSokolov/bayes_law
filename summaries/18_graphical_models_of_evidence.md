How can the structure of a body of legal evidence be drawn and computed with? The chapter moves from Wigmore charts to Bayesian networks, directed acyclic graphs with conditional probability tables, and explains the three elementary structures (chain, fork and collider), conditional independence, d separation and explaining away. It presents Kadane and Schum's probabilistic analysis of the Sacco and Vanzetti evidence (Commonwealth v Sacco and Vanzetti), including Bullet III, the ballistic comparison, the substitution defence and consciousness of guilt evidence, with a simplified network. It describes the idioms of Fenton, Neil and Lagnado (evidence and accuracy, cause and consequence, alternative explanation, opportunity and motive, witness dependence) and object oriented Bayesian networks. It then applies causal graphs and Pearl's structural causal models to tort causation: the but for test as a counterfactual computed by abduction, action and prediction on a twin network, overdetermination, the NESS test, and material contribution in mesothelioma cases such as Fairchild read as a collider problem. It closes by arguing that graphs are choices that reflect a theory of the case, which makes them contestable, and by comparing Wigmore charts, Bayesian networks and chain event graphs on the Kercher evidence. Exercises compute explaining away and the effect of witness collusion on a likelihood ratio.

## From the literature

Wigmore charts (Wigmore 1913; Anderson, Schum and Twining 2005). Bayesian networks (Pearl 1988) and causal inference with structural models, interventions and counterfactuals (Pearl 2009). The Sacco and Vanzetti analysis (Kadane and Schum 1996). Legal idioms for Bayesian networks (Fenton, Neil and Lagnado 2013). Object oriented networks for DNA (Dawid, Mortera and Vicard 2007). The NESS test (Wright 1985). Actual causation in structural models (Halpern and Pearl 2005). Fairchild and the mesothelioma cases, discussed in the tort chapter. The comparison of graphical methods on the Kercher case (Dawid and colleagues 2024). Bayesian networks in forensic science (Taroni, Biedermann, Bozza, Garbolino and Aitken 2014).
