This chapter treats the administrative state as a decision maker and asks how courts should review its decisions. It presents cost benefit analysis (expected value in government, uncertainty, and the precautionary principle), judicial review of costs and benefits, and deference to agencies after Loper Bright, with Chevron and its end and a Bayesian reading of deference. Cases include Chevron v Natural Resources Defense Council, Skidmore v Swift, Loper Bright Enterprises v Raimondo, Michigan v EPA and Business Roundtable v SEC. It then considers enforcement as a decision and the regulation of AI in law, including the EU AI Act. The chapter argues that deference can be read as a way of weighing an agency's expertise as evidence, and that cost benefit analysis under uncertainty should report distributions. Readers searching for regulatory impact analysis, OMB Circular A 4, Chevron deference, Skidmore weight, arbitrary and capricious review, the precautionary principle, or AI regulation will find the chapter's argument and its sources here.
