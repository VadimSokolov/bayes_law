This chapter applies expected utility theory to crime, punishment and the courts themselves. It sets out Becker's economic approach to crime and punishment: a potential offender offends if the expected gain exceeds the probability of conviction times the sanction, and the state chooses enforcement spending and sanctions to minimise the sum of the harm from crime and the costs of enforcement. It covers optimal fines and the probability and severity trade off, enforcers' incentives and corruption, the market for illegal goods, rational addiction, social interactions in crime, the value of a statistical life, and punitive damages and the multiplier. It then turns to the economics of litigation and settlement: why cases settle, the Priest and Klein selection of disputes for litigation, asymmetric information, who pays the costs (the English and American rules), plea bargaining, and the cost of the courts. Cases discussed include State Farm v Campbell and Twombly. Readers searching for deterrence theory, law and economics, optimal enforcement, punitive damages ratios, settlement models or plea bargaining models will find the chapter's argument and its sources here.
