What would a Bayesian program for the law look like in practice? This concluding chapter gathers the book's argument into ten proposals, each tied to earlier chapters: report likelihood ratios with ranges, direct juries on conditioning with the island problem and the Simpson argument, separate activity from source, proportionate remedies where causation is probabilistic (lost chances, clinical negligence, the Insurance Act 2015), judge investment representations by their implied distributions as in Greensill, combine market and expert evidence of solvency in fraudulent transfer and wrongful trading cases, use single joint experts who report distributions, triage by expected cost of delay, publish law as code where it is computation, and hold machine tools to Bayesian standards. It revisits objections to Bayesian legal reasoning, including the presumption of innocence as a prior of one over N and the view that law is a human practice, and closes on Lindley's expectation that all would be Bayesian by 2020.
