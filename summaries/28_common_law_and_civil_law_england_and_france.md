# Chapter 28. Common Law and Civil Law: England and France

This chapter compares English common law and French civil law as two solutions to the same problems of inference and decision under uncertainty. It treats the law on a question as an unknown function learned either from precedent (stare decisis, ratio decidendi, obiter dicta, distinguishing) or specified in advance by a code (Code civil, Article 5, jurisprudence constante), and models both with a hierarchical shrinkage model. It contrasts adversarial and inquisitorial procedure (juge d'instruction, cour d'assises, Crown Court jury) as ways of gathering and aggregating evidence, with a simple likelihood ratio model of advocates drawing from a pool of evidence and an argument for equality of arms. It covers free proof and intime conviction (Article 353 Code de procedure penale), reasoned verdicts after Taxquet v Belgium, French written proof rules, standards of proof, perte de chance against Gregg v Scott, general clause tort and strict liability (Articles 1240 and 1242, Jand'heur, Badinter law), the French ban on judge analytics in Article 33 of the 2019 justice reform law, the Catala language, and the convergence of the two traditions.

## From the literature

Dewatripont and Tirole (1999) on advocates; Gennaioli and Shleifer (2007) on the evolution of the common law; Priest (1977) and Rubin (1977) on the efficiency of the common law; Clermont and Sherwin (2002) on civil law standards of proof; Taxquet v Belgium (European Court of Human Rights, 2010); Gregg v Scott; Shavell (1987) on accident law; Merigoux, Chataing and Protzenko (2021) on Catala; Zweigert and Kotz (1998) on comparative law. The chapter applies the authors' own proportional loss of a chance argument (Polson and Sokolov 2026) to the French doctrine.
