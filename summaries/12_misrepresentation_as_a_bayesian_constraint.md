# Chapter 12. Misrepresentation as a Bayesian Constraint

How can the law of misrepresentation be read as a constraint linking what a speaker says to what the speaker's evidence warrants? The chapter models a statement as a signal whose force depends on the speaker's posterior, and maps doctrine onto the model. It covers fraud (Derry v Peek, Lord Herschell's three limbs), negligent misstatement (Hedley Byrne v Heller, Caparo v Dickman), the Misrepresentation Act 1967 section 2(1), opinions (Bisset v Wilkinson), intentions (Edgington v Fitzmaurice), half truths and changed circumstances (Dimmock v Hallett, With v O'Flanagan), inducement and reliance (Redgrave v Hurd), and insurance disclosure (Carter v Boehm, Marine Insurance Act 1906 section 18, Insurance Act 2015 duty of fair presentation). It links the topic to Bayesian persuasion and to proper scoring rules for assessing statements. The chapter argues that these doctrines constrain the gap between assertion and warranted belief, and that inducement means the statement moved the recipient's posterior in a way that mattered.
