# Chapter 26. Intellectual Property: Surveys, Obviousness and Machines

This chapter asks how intellectual property law uses probability and counterfactuals, and how artificial intelligence changes its doctrines. It treats trade mark likelihood of confusion and survey evidence, including the Lanham Act and Interflora v Marks and Spencer, and proposes a posterior for the net confusion rate after subtracting a control. It reads the obviousness test (Pozzoli v BDMO, Graham v John Deere, KSR v Teleflex, reasonable expectation of success, hindsight bias, secondary considerations) as a probabilistic judgment in which secondary considerations act as likelihood ratios. It covers patent damages: lost profits under the Panduit factors, reasonable royalties and the Georgia Pacific hypothetical negotiation, apportionment and conjoint surveys. It then considers machine inventorship (DABUS, Thaler v Comptroller General, Thaler v Vidal, USPTO guidance of 2025) and the skilled person equipped with AI tools. It ends with copyright and generative AI training, fair use, text and data mining, the Bartz v Anthropic settlement, and similarity evidence and apportionment for model outputs.
