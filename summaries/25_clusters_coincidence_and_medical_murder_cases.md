This chapter asks what a cluster of deaths around a nurse or doctor proves, and how statistics have both convicted the guilty and contributed to miscarriages of justice. Its cases are Lucia de Berk, where the one in three hundred and forty two million figure of Elffers was later discredited, Lucy Letby, Beverley Allitt and Harold Shipman. It explains why clusters happen by chance, the multiple comparisons problem and the Texas sharpshooter fallacy, the invalidity of multiplying p values across wards, the contamination of incident selection once suspicion has fallen on a person, and the misleading effect of shift charts. It gives a Bayesian analysis with a small prior, a likelihood ratio for the cluster, and separate likelihood ratios for medical evidence that allow for misinterpretation and dependence. It sets out the 2022 Royal Statistical Society safeguards, notes the questions on which the safety of the Letby convictions would turn without taking a view, and discusses wrongful convictions, exonerations and the Criminal Cases Review Commission.
