# Chapter 4. Classic Problems: Simpson, the Island and the Search

This chapter works three classic problems and the graphical tools that extend them. The first is the argument of Alan Dershowitz in the O. J. Simpson trial that prior domestic violence was nearly irrelevant because fewer than one battered woman in 2,500 is killed by her partner in a year. Following I. J. Good, the chapter shows that the relevant quantity conditions on the murder as well, and on the stated numbers gives a probability of about eight ninths, and it names the error the Dershowitz fallacy. It also discusses the criminal and civil verdicts in Simpson and the defence attack on laboratory contamination. The second problem is the island problem, treated coherently by Dawid and Mortera, with uncertainty about the match probability (the size biased mean), search strategies in which excluded inhabitants strengthen the case, and relatives. The third is how a suspect was found, including database searches and the dispute between Balding and Donnelly and the National Research Council report. The chapter then presents Jim Smith's event trees and chain event graphs, activity level propositions, object oriented Bayesian networks for forensic genetics, and the 2024 comparison of Wigmore charts, Bayesian networks and chain event graphs on the Kercher case.

## From the literature

The Simpson argument (Dershowitz 1996); Good's correction in Nature (1995) and Merz and Caulkins (1995); the island problem (Dawid 1994; Dawid and Mortera 1996; Balding and Donnelly 1995); the prosecutor's fallacy and the defense attorney's fallacy (Thompson and Schumann 1987); Balding and Donnelly on the prosecutor's fallacy in DNA cases (Criminal Law Review 1994); the National Research Council report on DNA (1996); Donnelly and Friedman (Michigan Law Review 1999); Stockmarr (1999) on database searches; chain event graphs (Smith and Anderson 2008; Collazo, Gorgen and Smith 2018); probabilistic expert systems for forensic genetics (Dawid and others 2002, 2007; Mortera, Dawid and Lauritzen 2003); Dawid, Dotto, Mortera, Graves, Kadane, Robertson, Smith and Wilson on Wigmore charts, Bayesian networks and chain event graphs in the Kercher case (2024); People v Simpson (Los Angeles Superior Court 1995).
